The Fall We Never Took

February 2026

This is a photo of my cofounder and me, mid-fall, tipping off a catamaran into the cold, bright water.

You would expect screams.

There were none.

We were laughing so hard we could barely breathe.

Why? Because for a moment, everything was simple. We were alive. We had chosen risk. And risk, when you choose it, tastes like freedom.

So we went to San Francisco for three months to build a robotics startup. We wanted more of that feeling — the fall, the laugh, the alive part.

But something strange happened.

We never fell.

We never laughed.

We were afraid to even step on the boat.

Anatomy of a Quiet Failure

We left full of hope. An adventure. All our savings poured into a one-way bet. No apartment. No plan. Just the belief that motion would create meaning.

Then we arrived.

And the question came, simple and brutal:

What do we do?

I wanted to build anything. Movement first, clarity later.

My cofounder wanted the opposite. He wanted the idea to be right before the first brick was laid.

He is a rare kind of mind — precise, generous, frighteningly sharp.

So we searched. Robotics labs. Home robots. Warehouse robots. New angles, new stories, new slides.

But we never truly began.

The False Start

We live in an age that whispers, constantly: Start something.

The internet is full of voices telling you that building a company is the highest form of life. Some of them are brilliant. They make creation feel inevitable. Necessary. Noble.

After a while, a quiet arrogance appears:

Why doesn't everyone do this?

But hidden in the advice is a contradiction.

"Just start. Build anything."

"Choose something you'll dedicate ten years to."

Both sound true. They cannot both be followed.

Starting a company is stepping into the largest decision space you will ever face — industry, product, timing, people, truth. The map is blank. The paths are infinite.

And this is where smart people get trapped.

The Intelligence Trap

There is an algorithm called Monte Carlo Tree Search. It simulates futures, explores possibilities, calculates the best move. Smart people love it. It works beautifully in school, in games, in bounded worlds.

But it has a weakness: when the space becomes too large, it collapses.

Entrepreneurship is that collapse.

Suddenly, there is no best decision. Only guesses.

You stand before infinite possible lives, and the weight of choosing freezes you.

Even the greatest Go players — and the strongest AIs — cannot compute their way through the game. At the highest level, they stop calculating and start feeling. They use intuition. Heuristics. Pattern, not proof.

At some point, you must accept this:

You do not control enough to be certain.

So you move anyway.

The Dumb Edge

When compute is scarce, systems rely on a simple signal: value. A fast, rough sense of direction. Not perfect, but usually right.

Call it instinct. Call it taste. Call it gut.

The people who succeed often share one trait: they decide quickly, and on average, correctly.

Not because they know more — but because they move more.

The Cost of Slowness

A CEO's real job is decisions.

And speed matters.

The true advantage of a startup is not intelligence. It is velocity. You can try instead of simulate. Act instead of debate.

Early on, your model of the world is mostly wrong anyway. Thinking longer doesn't fix that. Reality does.

Today, with AI, the cost of trying has collapsed. What once took weeks can be tested in hours. The rational move is no longer to think more — but to test faster.

We often spent days deciding what to do.

We should have just done it.

What Happened After Three Months

We pivoted again and again. Each time, hope diluted a little more. In San Francisco, you must always have a story, so we kept inventing new ones.

Eventually, the talking felt hollow. The exploration, circular. The motion, fruitless.

So we stopped.

And we went to work for other companies.

I won't lie. The disappointment is heavy. There is bitterness in it. A quiet grief for something that never quite existed.

But the sea is still there.

And somewhere inside, the call to jump remains — softer now, more careful, but unmistakably alive.
